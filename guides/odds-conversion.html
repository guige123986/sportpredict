Odds Conversion Guide: Decimal, Fractional & American

Understanding Odds Formats

Odds are the language of sports betting. They tell you two critical things: how much you can win and how likely an outcome is according to the bookmaker. However, odds are expressed differently around the world, and understanding each format is essential for any serious bettor. The three most common odds formats are decimal, fractional, and American, and they all express the same underlying information in different ways.

Knowing how to convert between these formats allows you to compare odds across different bookmakers and regions, calculate your potential returns accurately, and identify value opportunities. Whether you are looking at European sportsbooks that use decimal odds, British bookmakers with fractional odds, or American sportsbooks with moneyline odds, the math behind them is identical — only the presentation differs.

This guide breaks down each format, shows you the conversion formulas, and explains how to use our Odds Calculator to handle the conversions instantly.

Decimal Odds Explained

Decimal odds are the most straightforward format and the default on SportPredict. Popular across Europe, Australia, and Canada, decimal odds represent the total return per unit staked, including your original stake. For example, odds of 2.50 mean that for every $1 wagered, you receive $2.50 back if you win — $1.50 in profit plus your $1 stake returned.

The formula for calculating profit from decimal odds is simple: Profit = (Decimal Odds × Stake) − Stake. So a $100 bet at odds of 1.80 returns $180 total ($80 profit). Decimal odds of 2.00 represent an even-money bet, where you double your money. The lower the decimal odds, the more likely the outcome according to the bookmaker. Odds below 2.00 indicate a favorite, while odds above 2.00 indicate an underdog.

Fractional Odds Explained

Fractional odds are the traditional format used in the United Kingdom and Ireland. They express the ratio of profit to stake. For example, odds of 3/1 (read "three to one") mean you win $3 for every $1 staked, plus your original stake returned. Odds of 5/2 mean you win $5 for every $2 staked, or $2.50 for every $1.

To convert fractional odds to decimal, use this formula: Decimal = (Numerator / Denominator) + 1. So 3/1 becomes 4.00 in decimal, and 5/2 becomes 3.50. Fractional odds like 1/4 ("four to one on") indicate a heavy favorite — you only win $1 for every $4 staked. These are common in horse racing and when strong favorites are playing.

American Odds Explained

American odds (also called moneyline odds) use a plus and minus system centered around a $100 baseline. Negative numbers indicate the favorite and show how much you need to wager to win $100. Positive numbers indicate the underdog and show how much you win on a $100 bet.

For example, odds of −150 mean you must bet $150 to win $100 profit (total return $250). Odds of +200 mean a $100 bet wins $200 profit (total return $300). To convert American odds to decimal: for negative odds, Decimal = (100 / |American|) + 1, so −150 becomes 1.67. For positive odds, Decimal = (American / 100) + 1, so +200 becomes 3.00. American odds of −100 (or +100) equal decimal odds of 2.00 — an even-money proposition.

Implied Probability

Every set of odds implies a probability of the outcome occurring. Understanding implied probability is crucial for identifying value bets. The formula is: Implied Probability = 1 / Decimal Odds. So odds of 2.00 imply a 50% chance, odds of 3.00 imply 33.3%, and odds of 1.50 imply 66.7%. When the sum of implied probabilities for all outcomes exceeds 100%, the excess represents the bookmaker's margin or overround.

How Bookmakers Set Odds

Bookmakers employ teams of analysts and use sophisticated statistical models to estimate the true probability of each outcome. They then build a margin (typically 2-5% for major markets) into the odds to ensure profitability regardless of the result. The initial odds are called the "opening line," which then adjusts based on market action, injury news, weather, and other factors. Understanding how odds are set helps you spot when the market has moved too far in one direction, creating value opportunities.
